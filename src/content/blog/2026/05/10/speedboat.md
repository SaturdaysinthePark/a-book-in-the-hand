**Speedboat** by Renata Adler follows Jen, a fictional 1970s journalist in New York City. The novel is structured in an episodic nature with bursts of observations and thoughts. It's not meant to be a linear read, so we jump between times in her life at school, the Hamptons, at work, covering the news, etc.. It's more of a collage of observations of society and urban life. One might believe that Jen is a proxy for Renata, perhaps a bit cynical. 

There's a lot of shiny liftable observations that double as sharp life lessons:

>“People who are less happy, I find, are always consoling those who are more.”


Jen also makes observations like how a psychological or emotional state can be utilized or weaponized in a social setting:

>“Being neurotic seemed to be a kind of wild card, an all-purpose explanation.”

> “'Do you realize how angry you sound?' must be one of the most infuriating questions in the language.”

In the example about 'being neurotic', its a get out of jail free card. In the other example about sounding angry, its displacing the reality of the situation back on the person who is upset.

There are observations of the urban dweller, the person sucked into the city and its social and professional life, chasing what everyone else has, and nothing of your own:

>“For a while, I thought I had no real interests—no theater, concerts, museums, stamp collections. Only ambitions and ties to people.."

Jen also makes an acute observation that many societal labels and terms are used to draw people in, but in fact are rarely close to the reality of what that label eschews. A good example of this is her observation of 'intellectuals' and how they will meet at a dinner party and swap buzzwords as if they are conversing with actual intellect:

>“There were standard dinner-party code words: 'perception,' 'nuance,' 'vulnerability.' If you left the room, you missed the sequence, but the substance remained the same.”

> “That 'writers write' is meant to be self-evident. People like to say it. I find it is hardly ever true. Writers drink. Writers rant. Writers phone. Writers sleep. I have met very few writers who write at all.”

She sort of highlights that anyone is a writer, and just because you are 'labeled' a writer doesn't mean you are only a writer too. We come up with these fancy career labels that are prestigious, especially in the urban sphere, but the reality is usually quite a mud of experiences. I wonder what her thoughts would be on New York City now with new careers such as 'influencers' or 'content creators'. 

The novel succeeds in making me think "_yes this makes sense, I've observed this too._" from time to time. Especially as someone who has bounced in and out of am I person of my own interests or a person of social obligations. That made me think of **The Courage to be Disliked** as well, and this whole idea of am I reacting or acting in life for the sake of others or for my own sake.  

Its a short read, perhaps not for everyone, but I think worth it if you're in the mood at a coffee shop and just need to sip and read and see these fragments and observations play out. 

Lastly, a rant I felt like I agreed with:

> “'Literally,' in every single case, meant figuratively; that is, not literally. This film will literally grab you by the throat. This book will literally knock you out of your chair.”
