Machado de Assis published *The Posthumous Memoirs of Brás Cubas* in 1881, but it reads like something written in the past few years. A dead Brazilian aristocrat narrates his life from beyond the grave, dedicating his memoirs "to the worm who first gnawed on the cold flesh of my corpse." It centers on themes of privilege that invites laziness, failed romance, half-baked philosophy, and a narrator who can't believe anyone takes life seriously.

I picked this up after hearing multiple authors call it a foundational work of metafiction. Brás Cubas breaks the fourth wall constantly apologizing for wasting our time, suggesting we skip ahead, cutting chapters short with a shrug. For a book published in 1881, this is definitely not the norm in terms of structure or style.

## The court jester narrator

Brás Cubas is a self-absorbed, privileged, and petty man. His persona is more like a court jester, allowed to say what everyone's thinking but won't admit whether its among friends or the theater of a king's court. His tone is sarcastic and spiteful, especially when watching people perform. Absurd beliefs treated as gospel. Everyone staging elaborate shows to pretend life means something. He's astonished by it all, and that astonishment drives the book's humor.

Nearly 150 years later, this has never felt more relevant to how politics and media are performing in front of us today. Everyone takes themselves seriously while accomplishing very little for society. Brás sees through the masks of politics, and his commentary hits harder because he's speaking from a place of privilege and from the afterlife. He has nothing left to prove, and no reputation to protect, so theorietically that makes him more honest with a touch of spite. 

## Memory in fragments

In less than 300 pages, there are over 150 chapters, so the book feels very much like a series of bursts. The structure matches how memory works to some degree, but also some chapters are just rushed or non-existent in terms of content. 

We get a glimpse of his upbringing, but its detailed in highlights and milestones, especially his school years where he was reportedly rambunctious to his return to Brasil. Later, his affairs with Virgília and politics gets pages of detail, since its more recent and more central to his sense of personal tragedy. We remember our lives in bursts and fixations, not clean arcs, so that's the glimpse we get from Bras. 

## A failure of a life that started with privilege

The whole story and ending is quite sad. After tallying his life's failures of growing up priveleged in the upper class of Brasil, with no fame, no political success, no meaningful relationships, Brás concludes with a "small positive balance": he didn't have children, so he didn't "bequeath to any creature the legacy of our misery." Antinatalism delivered with deadpan finality.

Whether you read it as dark comedy or genuine philosophy, it's a blunt way to close a memoir. He's not asking for sympathy. He's stating what he considers fact: existence is suffering, and not reproducing is the only ethical choice he managed. I personally feel as if more people around me in today's day are choosing not to have children for some adjacently similar reasons such as not wanting to bring a child into this 'cruel world.' Perhaps that's not exactly the same as Brás Cubas, but its somewhat aligned to avoid suffering or cruelty.

## A well-read author and narrator

Machado de Assis was obviously a voracious reader, and he puts that on display throughout the book. Clear callbacks and influence from Cervantes and Voltaire, and references to Dante and Aristotle, this is a literary dream. As a character, Brás name drops these writers and philosophers and even quotes their works, sometimes it's to show off, other times it's to undercut his own pretensions by misapplying a reference. The literary allusions are ways for Brás to construct his identity as someone educated, cultured, part of an intellectual tradition in the upper class. Despite his education and privelege, nothing comes out of it for his life, its all for show.

## Ahead of its time

Machado de Assis was doing metafictional tricks before the term existed. The combination of formal experimentation, social critique, and actual humor holds up all these years later, this book reads like a modern novel.

I gave it four stars instead of five because the qualities that make it brilliant also work against it. The plot is nearly nonexistent—by design, but not always compelling. The fragmented structure occasionally feels aimless. The unreliable narrator conceit leaves you wondering what happened versus what Brás wants you to believe.

But those are minor. *The Posthumous Memoirs of Brás Cubas* is sharp and witty. If you're drawn to metafiction, self-aware narrators, or books that poke holes in societal pretensions, read this.
