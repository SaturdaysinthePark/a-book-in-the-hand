I've always wondered about the origin of our way of thinking, the inner voice, the processing we do. In fact, I'm usualyl astonished how some people claim not to have a consciousness or inner voice, I believe them, but I don't really understand it. I'm not sure this book answered all those questions, but I'm glad I ran into it and read it, because it was astonishing. *The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind* by Julian Jaynes is one of those books that comes up eventually when you look hard enough about topics such as the above.

The core theory is that consciousness, the kind we're familiar with, the inner narrative, the sense of an "I" observing from the inside, didn't exist in human beings much before 3,000 years ago. Before that, people were what Jaynes calls bicameral, their minds split between an executive part of the brain that issued commands and a part that obeyed that command. The commands were experienced as an auditory voice and heard as a "god". The person followed.

## Dismantling the thing you take most for granted

Before Jaynes can make his case, he has to dismantle your assumptions about what consciousness even is. This is the most compelling part of the book. He asks you to consider: when you're playing an instrument, or driving, or reading without "noticing" the grammar, are you conscious of those things? You're not, and yet, you're doing them. We can't be conscious of the periods when we're not conscious, which means we have no reliable sense of how continuous it actually is. We assume it's a constant stream. 

What stuck with me most is his point about memory. When you recall going swimming, you see yourself swimming, but you never actually experienced seeing yourself swimming from the outside. What you're retrieving is not stored images, it's a reconstruction of a plausible narrative built from what you were conscious of at the time. We do this constantly without realizing it.

After finishing that chapter, I started asking friends how they think. Do they experience thought in first person, from the inside looking out, or do they picture themselves from a third-person lens, like a camera? The variation is fascinating, and some say people who can picture themselves from a third-person lens are more self aware than those who recall memories in the first person.

## Language as the architecture of thought

The second chapter lays out what Jaynes believes consciousness is made of: spatiality (we arrange things mentally as if in space), excerption (pulling out what we focus on from the whole), the analog "I" that can observe our mental space, the metaphor of "me" that can move within it, narratization (we are constantly telling ourselves a story of what's happening), and conciliation (fitting new things into existing patterns to make them recognizable). It's all built from language and metaphor. 

And how langauge developed and transformed is deep, like the verb "to be" comes from Sanskrit *bhu*, meaning "to grow or make grow," while "am" and "is" derive from *asmi*, "to breathe." Even our most fundamental assertions about existence are borrowed from physical sensation. Language didn't just describe our inner life, it constructed the material that inner life runs on.

The argument that follows is that consciousness arrived after language was invented, not as a by-product, but as the actual mechanism. If you start with humans who had no symbols at all, and trace how language develops from posture to auditory signal to modifier to noun, and how each new word shapes attention and perception, the leap to consciousness emerging through language feels reasonable rather than radical.

I sort of thought of the metaphor acting like a file compression algorithm. A good metaphor of something will compress a complex thing into something quickly understandable, in a sense that's how language developed. The whole theory of how language developed made a lot of sense to me. We likely first had to make language as a necessity, perhaps to call out danger, and then modify that language to call out _specific_ danger, and direction of danger, etc... Eventually, language had to compress into better symbols or metaphor that could quickly communicate and be understood in order for information to travel and be absorbed effectively. As this evolved generation to generation, it also evolved in how our mind bulds its narrative. Without language, you can't have that inner monologue! This also made me think how people who speak a different language from me probably think completely differently, and in different speeds too. The language likely changes the way they process things. Even the differences in the order or structure of a sentence likely has a differentiating impact that separates two cultures.

## The Iliad as evidence

To show that earlier people thought differently, Jaynes turns to the _Iliad_. His argument is that the characters don't have an inner consciousness the way modern people do. Achilles doesn't decide to fight; his *thumos* compels him. Some other words that were used:
- _Thumos_ is what drives a person, the person doesnt do an activity, his thumps compels him to do it
- _Phrenes_ has to do with respiratory apparatus, it can hold fear or run out of strength
- _Kradie_ → kardia → cardiac, refers to heart, if the kradie beats loudly they are afraid
- _Etor_ → also heart, but maybe belly; mental functioning - etor that laughs with joy, when Lycaon’s plea to live is mocked, its his etor that is loosened to be made weak
- _Noos_, from noeo, to see; perception; we see with the minds eye
- _Psyche_ or psychein which is to breathe; often used for ‘life’ - span of time from birth to death- when spear stabs a characters heart and his psyche dissolves, that is end of his life

There are no words used for thinking or self-motivatd intention to do something, the way we mean it.

Jaynes acknowledges the Iliad was altered in transmission, from oral tradition into written form over centuries. That weakens the argument a little; it's hard to use a transcribed and reworked text as clean evidence for how its original composers experienced the world. But as part of a cumulative case alongside archaeology and cross-cultural comparison, it's a reasonable direction to look, even if not airtight.

## The voice and why it had authority

The core of the bicameral thesis is in the section on auditory hallucinations. Many people hear voices, and this is not exclusively a marker of illness. Jaynes argues these are vestiges of a very old cognitive structure. The bicameral person didn't have an inner deliberative process. When a situation demanded action or judgment, the other hemisphere generated a command heard as an external voice, recognized as a god, a dead chief, a king, or some type of leading authoritative figure.

What made the voice authoritative? Jaynes traces it through language. The word *obey* comes from the Latin *obaedire*, ob and *audire*, with *audire* being the root for hearing. To hear was literally to obey. The voice that spoke inside, the closest voice imaginable, coming from a figure of the highest status the person could conceive, would have carried absolute authority, not irrationally but structurally.

The idea that decision-making creates stress regardless of the outcome, that the mere fact of having to decide triggers a physical response, points toward something real. It's much easier to be driven by a single voice than to manage competing ones. Modern anxiety might partly be the cost of consciousness itself.

## The voice goes quiet

Later in the book documents the historical moment when the bicameral mind began to break down, roughly 1200-800 BC. Jaynes points to art from Mesopotamia around 1300 BC, specifically a stone carving of the Assyrian king Tukulti-Ninurta, depicted both standing and kneeling in prayer before an empty altar. The god is absent. The texts of the period are full of anguish at this silence.

When the voice receded, people had to find replacements. Prayer developed. Then intermediaries: half-animal figures, eventually birds, eventually angels. And on the other side, demons to explain unanswered prayers and persistent suffering. Then omens, casting of lots, augury, and spontaneous divination, finding meaning in the external world to fill the gap left by the internal voice. It's a coherent theory of why religious practice takes the forms it does.

## What's left of the bicameral mind today

The final section argues that vestiges of the bicameral mind are still with us. Schizophrenia, Jaynes suggests, is not a malfunction but something that comes from the old cognitive structure reasserting itself. Oracles like Delphi were societies institutionalizing the conditions to produce the god voice after it had stopped coming naturally. Hypnosis is a kind of induced bicameral state, where an external authority steps in to take over volition. And our compulsion toward authority figures, uniforms, titles, the way the right voice in the right context still feels like a command, is the old wiring that never quite got removed. This part of the book is more speculative, which might be why it captured less of my attention. That being said, the underlying question question it poses of why we still seem to need to be told what to do, is one that doesn't have a clean answer.

## Overall thoughts

This is a monumental book and one I expect to reference for a long time. The first half-ish is more propulsive, but the latter third had captured less of my attention. 

If you have any interest in consciousness, language, or how the mind developed across history, this belongs on your list.
